Explain how an ADC works:
• In an ADC hardware, all voltage signals go to something called a quantizer, which takes samples from the voltage signals to
. determine the overall value of the voltage at a specific point in time.
• After the quantizer the signal then go to the encoder which translates numerical values generated by the quantizer into digital
. signals (ones and zeros) to the micro-controller to then be received by the register.
• The time it takes for the quantizer to take samples to then send it to the encoder is called conversion time.
• The sample time must be way bigger than the conversion time in order to have enough time to convert
• If the sample time is not bigger than the convertion time then the digital engineer has to design something like a buffer in the 
. MCU in order to send these signals one after another in order.

Difficult (Interview Question):
-I have a mic that produces an analog message based on my voice, how do I record it and produce it again through a speaker using a MCU?
• First we have to convert the analog wave coming from the microphone into digital values using a calculated sampling rate.
• Once we have it sampled we then store it into a FIFO inside our memory.
• Once we have it stored we can then call that FIFO at any time and pass it to the DAC and produce the same message we stored.

-The more samples you take the higher the quality of the signal you're trying to capture.

How would you determine the amount of samples needed for a given analog signal and what's the constraints?:
• Since increasing the number of samples taken improves quality of the signal we treat samples as a train of impulses over time.
• The more you increase the value of samples the more the value multiplied by the time domain increases.
• And since time domain equals the frequency domain then rise in the value of time domain faces a rise in value in the frequency domain.
• Once the frequency domain is increased the convolution of the frequency domain is increased, this equation is called @FourierTransform.
• In the frequency domain, the train of impulses is represented as a single impulse with a constant value.
• The more the samples increase the further the placement of the F(S) becomes.
• In order for the message to not get corrupted, the value of (Fs-Fm) must be greater than Fm where Fm represents the original signal's
. frequency and Fs represents the sampling rate over time (1/Ts).
• In other words your sampling rate has to be twice the amount of the frequency you're measuring at the very least.

-Time for 1 sample (Ts) = 1/Fs = 1/2Fm.

How does a quantizer convert voltage levels into numbers?:
• A quantizer is simply a group of comparators that the voltage signal is imposed upon, each comparator has a voltage level, the
. comparator that matches the voltage level that's imposed on it returns its value.
• Every sample is rounded to its nearest value that exists in the quantizer, the difference between the actual value and the rounded
. value is called quantizer_error
• step size = (Vmax - Vmin)/(Number of levels) where the number of levels is the ADC resolution, or 2^(ADC register width)
• Maximum quantizer_error = (step size)/2


Summarize the major characteristics of ADC:
• Conversion time (Ts)
• Resolution (Number of bits in the ADC register)
• Vref (The maximum voltage the quantizer uses to create its steps (Vmax))
• Parallel vs Serial: ADC can either be single ended or differential ended 


-Example: calculate ADC voltage resolution for an ADC with 8-bit resolution measuring voltage range between -5V and 5V.
• Voltage resolution = (Vmax-Vmin)/(2^no.bits) = ( 5- (-5) )/(2^8) = 0.039 V = 39 mV

-Example: Calculate the D0-D7 output for an 8-bit ADC knowing that its Vref is 2.56 V and its analog input is 1.7 V.
• Voltage resolution = (2.56-0)/(2^8) = 10 mV
• Number of steps = 1.7 V/10 mV = 170 steps
• D0->D7 output = 170 in binary = 10101010
